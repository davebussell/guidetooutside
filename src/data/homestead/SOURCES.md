# Homesteading: sources

Every fact, number, breed trait, space allowance, timing and rule of thumb in the Homesteading section (`src/data/homestead/*`, `src/lib/homestead/*`, the pages under `src/app/(site)/homesteading/`) comes from a source below, read in October 2026 (reviewed date: `HOMESTEAD_REVIEWED` in `sources.ts`). The id in brackets is the key in `sources.ts`; the topic pages list the sources they draw on and cite them in their JSON-LD. Where sources disagree, the pages give both and say so. Local rules (bylaws, water rights, burning rules, registration) are never stated: each topic says what to check and with whom.

## Laying hens

- **[vtLaying]** Jacobs and Clauer, *Why Have My Hens Stopped Laying? 5 Factors that Impact Egg Production*, Virginia Cooperative Extension 2902-1097, 2022. https://pubs.ext.vt.edu/2902/2902-1097.html
  Hens need 14 hours of light to sustain laying; below 12 hours production decreases and frequently stops (October to February in the north); some recommend 16 hours; add light in the morning; at least 6 hours of continuous dark; a 4–5 W LED per 100 sq ft. Used for `LAYING_HOURS`, `LAYING_STOPS_HOURS` and the daylight panel.
- **[azLighting]** Wright, *Lighting Schedules for Arizona Backyard Laying Flocks*, University of Arizona Cooperative Extension az2185, 2026. https://extension.arizona.edu/publication/lighting-schedules-arizona-backyard-laying-flocks
  14–16 hours ideal; resume added light when daylight falls below 14 hours; no more than 16 hours (`MAX_LIGHT_HOURS`); not 24/7; light before sunrise so hens roost at sunset; adjust every couple of weeks; no added light while daylight exceeds 14 hours.
- **[osuCoop]** Annala, Tuck, Kerr, Hammond and Olson, *Living on the land: Backyard chicken coop design* (EC 1644), Oregon State University Extension, 2014, reviewed 2023. https://extension.oregonstate.edu/sites/default/files/catalog/auto/EC1644.pdf
  At least 3 sq ft per bird with a run, 8–10 sq ft without; 5–10 in of roost per bird, 2 ft up, 10 in apart; one 12 × 12 × 12 in nest box per four birds, roof sloped 45°; 1 × 2 in mesh buried 6 in to 1 ft, netting overhead, coop at least 1 ft off the ground, secure floor, motion light, no dense planting; passive ventilation, no draughts, no insulation needed.
- **[omafraHeat]** Dam, Ward and Creighton, *Heat stress in commercial layers*, Ontario Ministry of Agriculture, Food and Agribusiness, 2020 (updated 2026). https://www.ontario.ca/page/heat-stress-commercial-layers
  Comfort 21–25 °C; above 27 °C body temperature rises and feed intake drops; 28–35 °C harder to shed heat; egg production may fall above 32–35 °C, smaller eggs and poorer shells; panting less effective in humid heat; water intake rises. Used for `HOT_DAY_C` (30 °C) and the heat text.
- **[okstateWater]** *Water management key to helping chickens beat the heat*, Oklahoma State University Extension news, 10 July 2020. https://news.okstate.edu/articles/agriculture/2020/stotts_water-hot-chickens.html
  Heat stress generally begins at about 85 °F (29 °C); chill water to about 50 °F (10 °C) to lower body temperature.
- **[azHeat]** Wright, *Managing Backyard Chickens During Periods of Extreme Summer Temperature*, Small and Backyard Poultry (Cooperative Extension). https://poultry.extension.org/articles/poultry-management/managing-backyard-chickens-during-extreme-summer-temperatures/
  Mild, moderate and danger signs of heat stress; shade on the west side or mid-run; several shaded waterers with ice; feed early or late; collect eggs as soon as laid; emergency cooling in cool, not icy, water up to the neck.
- **[umdWinter]** *Winter Weather and Small Flocks*, University of Maryland Extension, 2022. https://extension.umd.edu/resource/winter-weather-and-small-flocks
  Sheltered adults need no added heat (chicks do); birds cannot break ice, check water several times a day, heated base or lamp; ventilation removes moisture and ammonia; fix draughts; deep litter insulates; flat wooden perches rather than metal or plastic pipe against frostbite.
- **[cdcPoultry]** *Backyard Poultry*, US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, 2025. https://www.cdc.gov/healthy-pets/about/backyard-poultry.html
  Salmonella; wash hands; do not kiss or snuggle birds; keep poultry out of the house; no handling of chicks by children under five; collect eggs often, discard cracked eggs, do not wash warm eggs, refrigerate, cook to 160 °F (71 °C).
- Breed pages, The Livestock Conservancy: **[lcChantecler]** Chantecler (Oka, Quebec, 1908–1918; cushion comb, almost no wattles, withstands cold, not for warm climates, up to 220 eggs, winter layer); **[lcBrahma]** Brahma (pea comb, least troubled by cold, poor in heat, lays October–May, up to 200 eggs, muddy feathered feet can freeze); **[lcWyandotte]** Wyandotte (rose comb, cold-hardy, hens about 6.5 lb); **[lcPlymouthRock]** Plymouth Rock (cold-hardy by early feathering, about 200 eggs, good sitters); **[lcSussex]** Sussex (does well in winter, not fond of heat, 200–250 eggs, broody); **[lcRir]** Rhode Island Red (hardy in marginal conditions, 200–300 eggs); **[lcAustralorp]** Australorp (250+ eggs, black plumage needs ample shade); **[lcLeghorn]** Leghorn (cold and heat tolerant, 150–320 white eggs, not broody, rose comb better in cold as the large single comb is prone to frostbite). URLs in `sources.ts`.
- **[nakedNeck]** Fernandes, Raymundo, Martins, Lordelo and de Almeida, *The Naked Neck Gene in the Domestic Chicken: A Genetic Strategy to Mitigate the Impact of Heat Stress in Poultry Production*, Animals 13:1007, 2023. https://doi.org/10.3390/ani13061007 (abstract read through Europe PMC)
  The bare neck increases heat dissipation; such birds adapt, perform and reproduce better in hot, humid climates, of particular interest in the tropics.

## Ducks

- **[lcCampbell]** Khaki Campbell Duck, The Livestock Conservancy. 250–340 eggs a year from 5–7 months; good in hot and cold climates (deserts reaching 100 °F, winters below 0 °F for weeks); active forager.
- **[lcRunner]** Runner Duck, The Livestock Conservancy. Good strains lay more than 250 white or blue-green eggs; the most active forager; does well in cold and hot climates.
- **[rvcDuck]** *Duck Care Sheet*, Royal Veterinary College Exotics Service, 2020. https://www.rvc.ac.uk/Media/Default/small-animal/documents/RVC-Duck-health-care-sheet-2020Aug.pdf
  1.5–2 m² per duck housed by day, 0.5–1 m² overnight; water deep enough to submerge the head and cover the body, paddling pool; diet by age (18–20 %, 15 %, 14–18 % protein, calcium; no bread, citrus or young-chick feed); up to 10 years; social, 4–6 females per drake; fox fencing with a 3 ft apron and overhang; cover concrete floors (bumblefoot); minimise contact with wild birds; the list of signs to call a vet for.

## Honeybees

- **[pnwBees]** Sagili and Burgett, *Evaluating Honey Bee Colonies for Pollination* (PNW 623), Pacific Northwest Extension, 2011. https://pollinators.msu.edu/sites/_pollinators/assets/File/pnw623.pdf
  Bees rarely fly below 55 °F (13 °C, `BEE_FORAGE_C`); some foraging 55–60 °F, moderate 60–65 °F, maximum from 65–70 °F (18 °C, `BEE_FULL_C`); strong colonies start at lower temperatures; midsummer start may wait for 70 °F; poor weather can stop foraging for 2–3 days.
- **[kangJung]** Kang and Jung, *Threshold Temperature for the Outdoor Flight Activity of Honey Bee, Apis mellifera under Overwintering Conditions*, Journal of Apiculture 39(4):317–322, 2024 (AGRIS record). https://agris.fao.org/search/en/records/67f4dcece01f7420ea853250
  Flight recorded only above 9.3 °C in winter; with UF/IFAS's 50 °F inspection limit, the basis of `BEE_FLIGHT_MIN_C` (10 °C).
- **[ufBees]** Ellis and Hammons, *Overwintering Honey Bee Colonies in Northern Climates* (ENY-161), UF/IFAS Extension, 2013, revised 2022. https://ask.ifas.ufl.edu/publication/IN1006/pdf
  About 90 lb of honey for a northern colony; 25,000–30,000 bees go into winter well, under 15,000 unlikely to survive freezing; cluster centre about 95 °F with brood; inspect only above 50 °F; lift test; autumn syrup 2:1 by volume; preparation from August or September in the far north.
- **[aaaaiStings]** *Stinging Insect Allergy*, American Academy of Allergy, Asthma & Immunology, 2026. https://www.aaaai.org/tools-for-the-public/conditions-library/allergies/stinging-insect-allergy
  The symptoms of anaphylaxis; a medical emergency that may be fatal; see an allergist after a serious reaction. (No dosing advice is given on the site.)

## Dairy goats

- **[isuGoats]** Tranel, *Beginner Dairy Goat Fact Sheet*, Iowa State University Extension, 2006. https://polk.extension.wisc.edu/files/2011/08/DairyGOATSFactSheet06.pdf
  1,200–2,600 lb milk a year; breed averages (Saanen 2,154, Alpine 2,134, Toggenburg 2,026, LaMancha 1,837, Nubian 1,797 lb); lactation averages 284 days; season late August to mid-March, mostly September–January; gestation 145–155 days; about 2 kids; first breeding at 70–80 lb or 7–10 months; 15–20 sq ft per doe.
- **[nmsuGoats]** Hagevoort, Smith and Rivera, *Housing and Working Facilities for Dairy Goats* (D-703), New Mexico State University, 2011. https://pubs.nmsu.edu/_d/D703/
  10–15 sq ft bedded area plus 25 sq ft exercise per goat; kidding pen at least 4 × 5 ft; 4 ft woven wire with board, barbed or electric wire on top, overhang; dry cold tolerated without draughts, wet draughty housing brings respiratory infection and hypothermia; shade in extreme heat; open shed facing south or away from the wind; warmed water in cold weather.
- **[adga]** *Breed Standards*, American Dairy Goat Association. https://adga.org/breed-standards/ Alpine thrives in any climate (30 in, 135 lb); LaMancha developed in the US, produces well in a variety of climates; Nigerian Dwarf 17–22.5 in, high butterfat; Nubian high butterfat and protein; Saanen high production, calm.
- **[osuSaanen]**, **[osuToggenburg]**, **[osuNubian]** Oklahoma State University, Breeds of Livestock. Saanen sensitive to heat and sun, best in cooler conditions, shade essential, 3–4 % fat; Toggenburg best in cooler conditions, 3.7 % fat; Anglo-Nubian the dairy breed best suited to hot conditions, used to improve goats in tropical countries, less milk, 4–5 % fat, longer breeding season.
- **[defraGoats]** *Code of recommendations for the welfare of livestock: Goats*, Defra, 1989. Gregarious, prefer social groups, a lone goat needs more contact; fencing at least 1.2 m; water at all times; covered dry lying area; foot trimming where needed; electric mesh unsuitable for horned goats and kids.
- **[bgsGoats]** *Keeping Goats: Basic Requirements*, British Goat Society. Suffer in wind, incessant rain, hot sun and flies; soaked goats risk pneumonia; at least 2.5 m² for a dairy doe in a single pen; milk at least once a day, twice for six weeks after kidding.
- **[cdcRawMilk]** *Raw Milk*, CDC, 2025. https://www.cdc.gov/food-safety/foods/raw-milk.html The germs raw milk can carry, the groups most at risk, and pasteurisation as the protection.

## Meat rabbits

- **[osuRabbits]** Vanderzanden, Tuck, Kerr, Hammond and Olson, *Raising Rabbits for Meat: Providing Basic Care* (EC 1655), Oregon State University Extension, 2018. Gestation 28–35 days, average 31; wean at two months; shade the nest box and ventilate in heat.
- **[usuRabbits]** Okoh and Hadfield, *Rabbit Breeding and Management: A Guide for Producers*, Utah State University Extension, 2025. Gestation 28–33 days; wean days 42–55; above 85 °F heat stress and reduced male fertility; a 35-day interval between litters.
- **[usdaRabbits]** Casady, Sawin and Van Dam, *Commercial Rabbit Raising* (Agriculture Handbook 309), USDA ARS, 1966, revised 1971. https://ageconsearch.umn.edu/record/322040/files/ah309rev.pdf New Zealand White and Californian the preferred white meat breeds; Californian 8–10.5 lb, Champagne d'Argent 9–12 lb; medium breeds bred at 5–6 months; gestation 31–32 days, 98 % kindle on days 30–33; about 8 young; wean at 8 weeks; hutch no deeper than 2.5 ft, 2 ft high, 3–4 ft long; hot sun harmful, shade, sprinklers, ventilation; rebreed at 42 days to skip extreme months; winter litters survived −15 to −20 °F in insulated boxes with a wool nest; check nest boxes daily for 3–4 days; a doe with seven 8-week-old young drinks about a gallon a day.
- **[faoRabbits]** Lebas, Coudert, Rouvier and de Rochambeau, *The rabbit: husbandry, health and production*, chapter 6, FAO, 1986. https://www.fao.org/4/x5082e/X5082E0e.htm Temperature regulation works 0–30 °C; stretch out above 25–30 °C; heat prostration at 35 °C and above (`VERY_HOT_DAY_C`); newborns need at least 28 °C, nest centre 30–35 °C, can die of cold 10 cm from the litter; humidity below 55 % and hot dry air are hard on rabbits; ammonia 20–30 ppm damages airways, limit 5 ppm; water tank in shade; sore hocks at 31–32 °C on wire.

## Rainwater

- **[twdbRain]** *The Texas Manual on Rainwater Harvesting*, third edition, Texas Water Development Board, 2005. https://www.twdb.texas.gov/publications/brochures/conservation/doc/RainwaterHarvestingManual_3rdedition.pdf
  0.62 US gallons per sq ft per inch (1 L per m² per mm); installers assume 75–90 % efficiency, the worked example uses 85 % (`RUNOFF`); collection area is the roof footprint; tiles and composite shingles lose about 10 %; first flush 1–2 gallons per 100 sq ft (`FIRST_FLUSH_L_PER_M2`) or 10 gallons per 1,000 sq ft; storage through the longest spell without rain; the monthly water balance method (`roofWater` storage); median versus average rainfall (Dallas 35.0 against 29.3 in); potable systems need filtration and disinfection in series (5-micron, 3-micron carbon, UV), filter and lamp replacement, regular testing; composite shingles not for drinking water; no lead solder; maintenance duties.
- **[cdcRain]** *Collecting Rainwater and Your Health: An Overview*, CDC, 2024. https://www.cdc.gov/drinking-water/about/collecting-rainwater-and-your-health-an-overview.html
  Rainwater is not necessarily safe to drink without removing germs and chemicals; sources of contamination; test regularly, ask the health department; use tap or bottled water for drinking, cooking, teeth, rinsing produce and watering food plants; first-flush diverter; screen or empty barrels every 10 days; keep rainwater out of drinking-water lines; rules vary by state.
- **[psuRainBarrel]** Yencha, *Rain Barrel Winter Maintenance: Don't Forget to Disconnect!*, Penn State Extension, 2022. Drain and detach before the median first hard freeze (below 28 °F for an extended time), reconnect after the last; water expands about 9 % when it freezes; store upside down or under cover, strapped; downspout extension; winter cleaning and repairs.

## Wood heat

- **[epaBurn]** *Best Wood-Burning Practices*, US EPA Burn Wise, 2024. https://19january2025snapshot.epa.gov/burnwise/best-wood-burning-practices Below 20 % moisture; season at least six months; outdoors, off the ground, top covered only; moisture meter; signs of seasoned wood; what not to burn; little smoke from a good stove.
- **[lwfFirewood]** *Scheitholz: Produktion, Lagerung, Kennzahlen* (Merkblatt 20), Bavarian State Institute of Forestry (LWF), 2025. https://lwf.bayern.de/mam/cms04/service/dateien/mb20_scheitholz_2025_rz_web_bf.pdf
  Net heating values at 0/15/20/30/50 % water content (conifer 5.20/4.32/4.02/3.44/2.26, broadleaf 5.00/4.15/3.86/3.30/2.16 kWh/kg; `HEAT_AT_20`, `HEAT_DRY`); air-dry about 4 kWh/kg, fresh about 2; 0.7 solid m³ per stacked m³ (`SOLID_PER_STERE`); worked figures for beech 1,885, birch 1,777, pine 1,516, spruce 1,333 kWh per stere (`LWF_REFERENCE`); fell in winter and split soon; unsplit wood up to two years; beech and spruce dry below 20 % within a year; storage advice; species notes (oak, ash, maple, hornbeam, cherry, birch, poplar, willow, pine, spruce). The firewood table's method.
- **[gwdd]** Zanne et al., *Global Wood Density Database* (data for Chave et al. 2009, *Towards a worldwide wood economics spectrum*, Ecology Letters 12:351–366), Dryad, https://doi.org/10.5061/dryad.234, read through the `wdData` table in the R package BIOMASS 2.2.7-1 (CRAN). Basic density (oven-dry mass per green volume) for the 43 species in `firewood.ts`, the mean of the database's records per species. The North American values agree with the green specific gravities in the USDA Forest Products Laboratory *Wood Handbook* (FPL-GTR-190, 2010), Table 5-3a.
- **[ukyFirewood]** *Comparison of Properties Affecting Firewood*, University of Kentucky Forestry. Gross heat per cord at 20 % moisture (white oak 27.9, hickory 29.3, sugar maple 25.9, white pine 15.0 million BTU), for the comparison with the net figures; air-dry at least six months.
- **[munalula]** Munalula and Meincken, *An evaluation of South African fuelwood with regards to calorific value and environmental impact*, Biomass and Bioenergy 33(3):415–420, 2009 (AGRIS record): blue gum, pine and acacias among the fuelwoods common in the Western Cape. **[wattleEthiopia]** *Acacia mearnsii*, Useful Trees and Shrubs of Ethiopia (World Agroforestry): black wattle used for firewood and charcoal, grown in woodlots, can become a weed. Basis for listing these trees under Africa.
- **[sfrsChimney]** *Chimney Safety, Stoves and Carbon Monoxide*, Scottish Fire and Rescue Service, 2024. Sweep wood-burning chimneys quarterly when in use (against the BC toolkit's once a year: both are shown); signs of a chimney fire; what to do; CO detector in every room with a fuel-burning appliance; wood dried 6–12 months, at or below 20 %; avoid long low-output burns, burn hot 30 minutes after one.
- **[bcHeating]** *Heating fire safety toolkit*, Province of British Columbia, Office of the Fire Commissioner. Licensed cleaning and inspection of chimney and vents every year; anything that can burn 1 m from a heat source; ashes in a covered metal container at least 10 ft from buildings; test CO alarms monthly.
- **[cpscCo]** *Carbon Monoxide Information Center*, US Consumer Product Safety Commission. Symptoms; an alarm on every level and outside sleeping areas; get outside and call 911.
- **[hcCo]** *Carbon monoxide: Overview*, Health Canada, 2024. Wood stoves and fireplaces produce CO; blocked flues; CO can affect you before you notice; leave if the alarm sounds.
- **[usdaFirewood]** *Baby, it's cold outside. Time to stock up on firewood.*, USDA, 2015. Asian longhorned beetle, emerald ash borer and gypsy moth spread in firewood; buy where you burn or heat-treated; APHIS works with states on quarantines.
- **(Erbs et al. 1983, no registry id: not reached)** D. Erbs, W. Beckman, S. Klein, *Estimation of degree-days and ambient temperature bin data from monthly-average temperatures*, ASHRAE Journal 25(6), 1983, 60–65 (citation as given in the reference list of a later degree-day review on core.ac.uk; the paper itself was not reached). The `erbsHdd` formula and its σm correlation, checked on NOAA's 1991–2020 monthly normals ([noaaNormals]): within 1 % of NOAA's heating degree days at nine of eleven US stations, 2 % at Savannah, 5 % at Jacksonville. The page's figures use it from 11 November 2026, in place of the Met Office day formula on normal highs and lows, which the audit of 10 November found 13–44 % low in mild towns.
- **[noaaNormals]** NOAA NCEI, U.S. Climate Normals 1991–2020, monthly: MLY-TAVG-NORMAL, MLY-HTDD-NORMAL and MLY-TAVG-STDDEV for Boston Logan, New York Central Park, Philadelphia, Washington Reagan, BWI, Atlanta Hartsfield, Savannah, Raleigh State University, Richmond, Charlotte and Jacksonville International, read 11 November 2026 through the NCEI access-data service.
- **[metOfficeHdd]** Deroubaix et al., *Large uncertainties in trends of energy demand for heating and cooling under climate change*, Nature Communications 12:5197, 2021, Table 1 (the UK Met Office degree-day equations; full text through Europe PMC). The `metOfficeHdd` formula. Also read: Hyde, *Degree-days for heating calculations*, British Gas Corporation, 1980 (AIVC airbase 857, https://www.aivc.org/sites/default/files/airbase_857.pdf): the Met Office method from daily maximum and minimum, unreliable below a base of about 10 °C.

## Compost and manure

- **[uriCompost]** *Composting Livestock Manure*, University of Rhode Island Cooperative Extension. C:N 25:1–30:1 (20:1–40:1 works); 40–60 % moisture, wrung-out sponge; 130–150 °F; straw bedding helps, heavy wood bedding raises the ratio; site like manure storage, covered or roofed, runoff to grassed strips.
- **[cgsbOrganic]** Organic Federation of Canada, *Comparison between CAN/CGSB-32.310-2015 amended 2018 and CAN/CGSB-32.310-2020, Section 5 – Crop production* (read 11 November 2026). Clause 5.5.2.5: raw manure incorporated at least 120 days before harvest of crops whose edible parts touch the soil or soil particles, 90 days for other crops for human consumption; the same in both editions and in the 2025 public-review draft.
- **[usdaManure]** *Soil Building: Manures and Composts*, USDA Agricultural Marketing Service (National Organic Program). Raw manure at least 120 days before harvest when the edible part touches soil, 90 days for other food crops; compost from C:N 25:1–40:1, 131–170 °F for 3 days (in-vessel or static aerated) or 15 days with at least five turnings (windrow).
- **[epaCompost]** *Composting At Home*, US EPA. Greens and browns; what not to compost; two to three times as much brown as green by volume; 4–8 in of browns over scraps; wrung-out sponge; site; 4–6 in base of bulky browns; cure four weeks once heating stops; ready in 3–5 months.

## Root cellars and food storage

- **[umnStorage]** Tong, *Harvesting and storing home garden vegetables*, University of Minnesota Extension (reviewed 2026). Cool and dry 50–60 °F, 60 %; cold and dry 32–40 °F, 65 %; cold and moist 32–40 °F, 95 %; potatoes 32–40 °F, 95 %, 6 months, cure 50–60 °F for 14 days; onions cure at room temperature 2–4 weeks; carrots 8, beets and cabbage 5 months; pumpkins suffer below 45 °F.
- **[purdueStorage]** Lerner and Dana, *Storing Vegetables and Fruits at Home* (HO-125), Purdue Extension. Furnace basements too warm; north or east corner without pipes; unheated dirt-floored basement ideal; window for cooling, no light; vents open when outside is colder, closed when warmer; thermometers in the coldest spot and outdoors; humidity gauge; perforated polyethylene bags; apples and pears apart (ethylene); late potatoes 40–45 °F, 90 %; onions and garlic 32 °F, 65–70 %; squash 50–55 °F, 50–75 %; ½ in hardware cloth against rodents. Potatoes: the two sources disagree and both are shown.

## Season extension

- **[ncsuSeason]** Gunter, *Appendix E. Season Extenders and Greenhouses* (AG-831), NC State Extension, 2022. Row covers and low tunnels 4–5 °F frost protection; up to a month earlier or later for warm-season crops; venting above 90 °F; high tunnel construction and film lifespans; cold frame materials and siting; plastic pipe inadequate for snow and wind loads; anchoring.
- **[wsuSeason]** Kintner, *Season Extenders*, WSU Extension Master Gardeners, Skagit County, 2023. Light covers no frost protection; medium to 28 °F, 85 % light; heavy to 24 °F, 35–50 % light; water-filled cloches to about 16 °F; low tunnels 2–3 ft; as much as two to four weeks in spring and autumn (`COVER_WEEKS`); pin covers down.
